S001	This article explores how Aussie slang and idioms reflect national identity and challenge traditional ideas of what counts as “English”.
S002	From “larrikin” to “dinkum”, this is the tale of how Australians have made the English language their own.
S003	Is Australian English really English?
S004	The unique way Australians communicate has led to many people, particularly new Australians, asking this question.
S005	What is “grouse”?
S006	(it means very good)
S007	Who’s a “mate”?
S008	(in Australia, anyone can be).
S009	“Crikey” (wow)!
S010	This is confusing.
S011	But it is definitely English.
S012	The limits of “English” and its stories
S013	English is not just a language - it is a collection of stories.
S014	If we listen closely enough to English, we hear the clashing and clanging of Viking axes and swords.
S015	We hear the rowdy revelry of fancy French feasts.
S016	We hear a galloping American cowboy yell “hee-haw”.
S017	The people who meet English leave traces of themselves in its words.
S018	Rampaging Vikings gave English speakers a “scare” (originally a Viking word).
S019	“Fancy” French “nobles” gave us “cuisine” (all French words).
S020	American cowboys gave us “tall tales” - colourful stories too outlandish to be true.
S021	Like tall tales, new English words test the limits of what we are willing to accept as true.
S022	Australia and its words have stretched the credibility of English more than most.
S023	US writer Mark Twain once wrote,
S024	Australian history...does not read like history, but like the most beautiful lies.
S025	Mark Twain If Aussie words tell stories, those stories are told with a sly wink and smile.
S026	Embracing and creating Australia’s English stories Aussie ways of speaking are wrapped up in our history and our relationships.
S027	One underlying theme in the Australian experience has been rejecting British formality and hierarchy.
S028	At first, the British mocked those born in the Australian colony.
S029	The British called them “currency” (a unit of money that had no value outside of the colony).
S030	The British called themselves “sterling” (a unit of money valuable throughout the British Empire).
S031	But Aussies soon took pride in their status as “currency lads”.
S032	Over the next 100 years, this obsession with being informal was embedded in Australia.
S033	The word “afternoon”?
S034	Too long - Australians say “arvo”.
S035	The word “Australian”?
S036	- it’s “Aussie”.
S037	Many British words got swept up into the Aussie experience.
S038	Where a word like “bloody” was a rude insult in Britain, it became so commonly used in Australia to describe things, both good and bad, that it became known as “the great Australian adjective”.
S039	In Britain, the “larrikin” was “a mischievous or frolicsome youth”.
S040	In Australia, the “larrikin” started off as an awful gang member, but soon evolved into a fun, likeable person who disregarded rules and authority.
S041	This embrace of the “larrikin” represents how Aussies love an underdog story.
S042	These stories - or yarns - have been key to the Aussie experience.
S043	We even tell yarns about our English words to give them a more Aussie feel.
S044	“Dinkum”, meaning genuine, honest, or true, started off as British word meaning “hard work”.
S045	Not content with these British origins, Aussies made up a few yarns linking “dinkum” to the goldfields.
S046	One story links it to the Chinese goldminers - by some accounts deriving from a Cantonese phrase “ding kam” (頂金) meaning “real gold”.
S047	Another story attributes it to drinking practices among goldfields gamblers, who would agree to engage in “fair drinking”, or “fair dinkum”, as it was reputedly said in migrant-accented English.
S048	These goldfields stories are almost certainly not true - Aussie slang “dinkum” isn’t noted until the 1890s.
S049	However, we’ve come to learn that sometimes that accuracy of a story is not that important.
S050	Sometimes the more stories about a word, accurate or not, the more likely it is to survive.
S051	And Aussie words and stories - even when they are humorous or improbable - are important to how we relate to one another.
S052	Aussie relationships are forged - idealistically at least - through a sense of fairness, dry wit and colourful idioms.
S053	Aussies celebrate the “fair go”, which has evolved into a “fair crack of the whip”, “fair suck of the sav” or “fair shake of the sauce bottle” (if you ask former Prime Minister, Kevin Rudd).
S054	These humorous idioms are important to the way Aussies interact.
S055	Aussies may not say directly that a person is being “silly” or “stupid”, but they might say that person is “a sanger short of a picnic” or “three Tim Tams short of a packet” (“sanger” is a classic Aussie shortening for sandwich; “Tim Tams” a classic Aussie product).
S056	The future of Australian English Is Australian English really English?
S057	Almost certainly.
S058	Australian English is defined by its past stories which are fun, fair and funny - but most of all, not British.
S059	And, yet, Australian English is also defined by us - new Australians and old, and the way we relate to one another.
S060	A few years ago, my young son spotted a “galah”.
S061	He asked, “Isn’t galah a way of saying stupid?”
S062	I laughed and said, “Yep”.
S063	He asked, “Do Americans know that?”.
S064	“Most of them wouldn’t”.
S065	My 9-year-old smiled, “That’s the best thing about Australian words.
S066	They’re our words and other people don’t know them”.
S067	But he also added, “I think that bird should be called a ‘pink-atoo’.
S068	It looks like a pink ‘cockatoo’”.
S069	I am not sure that “pink-atoo” will ever catch on.
S070	But Australia English - and its collection of stories - is bound to keep growing.
