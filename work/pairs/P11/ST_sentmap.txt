S001	WASHINGTON — In July, Keir Starmer became British prime minister just one day after his party swept parliamentary elections.
S002	Donald Trump, who won the November 5 U.S. election, must wait 76 days to become president again.
S003	What gives?
S004	Britain’s opposition party, like its counterparts in some parliamentary democracies, runs a “shadow government" that is ready to seize power after winning an election.
S005	The United States has no such system.
S006	America’s president-to-be starts from scratch, tasked with filling posts for a sprawling government bureaucracy with a nearly $7 trillion budget and 3.5 million civilian and military personnel, including thousands of presidential appointees.
S007	“It is a mammoth task to get ready to govern,” said Valerie Smith Boyd, president of the Center for Presidential Transition.
S008	The presidential transition — those critical 11 weeks between Election Day in November and Inauguration Day in January — is a cornerstone of American democracy.
S009	The period is designed to ensure a seamless and peaceful transfer of power from one administration to another.
S010	“The idea is that they should come into their jobs and be ready to work on day one,” said Michael Shurkin, a director at consultancy 14 North Strategies who worked as a CIA analyst during the transition from George W. Bush to Barack Obama in 2008-09.
S011	The transition period used to be even longer — four months — and a rather low-key event throughout most of America’s history, according to historian Russel Riley.
S012	Riley has traced the earliest mention of the phrase “presidential transition” to 1948, arguing in The Washington Post that the process took on greater significance in the 1960s because of “the risks to a presidency — and indeed to the nation — of not having the newcomer and his team fully prepared from day one to meet the world’s challenges.”
S013	Tradition and law
S014	Today, the transition is a highly elaborate, formalized process, governed as much by tradition and custom as by laws and regulations.
S015	“It’s very much the customs and habits that candidates and the federal government do,” said Derek Muller, a Notre Dame law professor and expert on presidential transition.
S016	Some of the traditions are largely symbolic.
S017	For example, there's the post-election White House meeting between a sitting president and a president-elect.
S018	President Harry Truman is credited with encouraging this tradition by inviting Dwight Eisenhower, a bitter political rival, to the White House after Eisenhower’s 1952 victory.
S019	The example was followed by every president since, except for Trump after his 2020 defeat.
S020	Other practices are more consequential.
S021	In a tradition that dates to 1968, the president shares a copy of his daily intelligence briefing document — the President’s Daily Brief — with the incoming commander-in-chief.
S022	Neither practice is written into law, but much else about the transition is legally mandated.
S023	The Presidential Transition Act of 1963, updated over the years, formalizes some of the processes and mechanisms for a peaceful transfer of power.
S024	Among other things:
S025	Six months before the election, the president establishes a transition coordinating council, while each federal agency designates a transition director.
S026	By September 15, agency heads must lock in succession plans for noncareer employees.
S027	By October 1, the General Services Administration — the federal government’s landlord — enters into agreements with transition teams before providing them with office space and other resources.
S028	Although these are recent additions to the law, an orderly presidential succession has been the norm through most of American history.
S029	Then came the rupture of 2020: Trump, then running for reelection, claimed voter fraud and refused to concede.
S030	President Joe Biden was initially denied the daily intelligence briefing, and it took the GSA three weeks to “ascertain” him as the winner.
S031	Breaking with tradition, Trump didn’t invite Biden to the White House and left Washington without attending his inauguration.
S032	With Trump swiftly declared the winner of this year’s election, the pendulum has swung back toward established protocol.
S033	On Wednesday, Biden hosted Trump at the White House, where they posed for the cameras and pledged a smooth transition.
S034	The White House called their two-hour meeting “very cordial, very gracious and substantive."
S035	Filling 4,000-plus jobs
S036	A White House meet-and-greet isn’t the president-elect’s main mission.
S037	Topping the list of priorities: staffing a new administration.
S038	The incoming administration must fill more than 4,000 positions, with 1,200 requiring Senate confirmation, according to the Center for Presidential Transition.
S039	Current political appointees typically resign before or on the day the incoming president is inaugurated.
S040	This massive recruitment effort takes time and resources.
S041	Candidates for political positions must be interviewed, vetted and in some cases receive top-secret security clearances.
S042	While presidents-elect typically name a few dozen key players by Inauguration Day, most appointments come after they take office.
S043	Trump has already picked key administration officials: chief of staff, national security adviser, CIA director, director of national intelligence, attorney general, secretary of defense and secretary of state.
S044	More top-level personnel announcements are expected in the coming days and weeks.
S045	But while these announcements dominate the news cycle, the real work of presidential succession often takes place behind closed doors.
S046	Agency-level teams from both administrations work quietly to share information and orchestrate the complex handover.
S047	“This is what’s happened time and time again, with the Republican exception of 2020-2021,” Shurkin said in an interview with VOA.
S048	“The intention is to make sure that certainly people in, let’s say, the more vital elements of the government, particularly, say, Defense or CIA … don’t show up looking around and asking, 'What is this?
S049	What’s your job?
S050	How does anything work?'
S051	”
S052	The extent of ongoing coordination between the Trump and Biden teams remains uncertain.
S053	Biden has ordered his administration to facilitate a smooth handover, but Trump's team has missed key deadlines for agreements with the White House and GSA, making it difficult for federal agencies to fully share information with it.
S054	Muller said that while Trump's team might have privacy concerns, these delays carry real risks.
S055	“If you’re not able to access those federal agencies, you might come in a step slow about how you’re supposed to take over the apparatus of the federal government,” Muller said in an interview.
S056	'Lame duck' period
S057	The presidential transition is sometimes called the “lame duck” period because the outgoing president has diminished influence as he awaits the incoming president's inauguration.
S058	While the sitting president keeps full powers, including the pardon power, until January 20, the ability to implement major policies and decisions is limited.
S059	This creates a high-stakes chess game in domestic and global affairs.
S060	Allies and rivals alike must thread the needle between current and future administrations.
S061	Foreign governments, especially those with critical ties with the U.S., must walk a diplomatic tightrope.
S062	The diplomatic dance has already begun.
S063	While foreign governments maintain relations with U.S. embassies, Muller noted that foreign leaders are already reaching out to Trump “to feel him out in some of his positions, to think about how things might look under a new administration.”
S064	Earlier this week, Indonesian President Prabowo Subianto called Trump and suggested the two meet in person.
S065	“You’re dealing with some significant changes about how the United States is going to govern, and so it’s a delicate balancing act for those foreign government leaders in the international context,” Muller said.
